## 3. Content style (as publicly observable)

No article, interview or press piece *describes* TGB's visual style or reel format, so I could not verify it. What can be seen:

- **Short, first-person trust and prayer lines (VERIFIED via the account's own posts, listed in section 5).** Typical lines are under 12 words, use plain language and address God directly ("Dear God…", "…I trust you Lord").
- **Scripture captions (VERIFIED).** The IG and Threads bio uses Psalm 25:1, "In you, Lord my God, I put my trust." A TikTok from the apparel account quotes Isaiah 60:22 with hashtags #scripture #bible #god #christian #trustgodbro.
  - Source: https://www.tiktok.com/@trustgodbroapparel/video/7218041506110639406 (date not shown; extract says ~2,746 likes)
- **Music (unverified).** One search summary said TikTok results show prayer and scripture videos "often with worship music." That came from a summarizer, not a specific source.
- **Tone (INFERENCE).** Reassuring and colloquial, as signalled by the "Bro" brand name. The themes are trust, God's timing, endurance through pain, and putting God first.
- **Typography (VERIFIED for the logo only).** The trademark describes "TRUST GOD BRO in all capital letters in stylized font resembling black paint brush markings over a white canvas." Typography inside posts was not verified.
- **Post format** (text-on-footage, voiceover, static graphics): **NOT VERIFIED.**

## 6. Why faith quote accounts get shared (broader context)

- **Religious cues raise engagement and promotional cues lower it (VERIFIED, peer-reviewed).**
  - Myers, Syrdal, Mahto & Sen, "Social religion: A cross-platform examination of the impact of religious influencer message cues on engagement," *Technological Forecasting and Social Change* vol. 191 (2023).
  - Method: about 58K posts from Christian influencers on Facebook, Twitter and Instagram.
  - Findings: religious cues increased engagement, promotional cues decreased it, and the effect of hashtags and mentions varied by platform.
  - Sources: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0040162523001270 ; https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/tefoso/v191y2023ics0040162523001270.html
- **Audience base (VERIFIED as cited in that study's extract).** Pew data: 20% of Americans say they share their faith online, and 46% view religious content online in a typical week.
- **Practitioner guidance (VERIFIED, anecdotal; specific page URLs not pinned down).** Church-marketing guides say short verse or quote reels are easy to make because no one has to appear on camera, and that people "save and share" them because they are encouraging. They also say saves are a strong ranking signal and that trending audio extends reach.
  - Examples: https://www.resourceumc.org/content/instagram-reels-ideas-for-churches ; https://www.churchofengland.org/resources/digital-labs/blogs/how-make-most-instagram-reach-your-audience-2024 (exact attribution of each point not confirmed)
- **Gen Z demand (VERIFIED as reported, secondary).** A Substack piece cites American Bible Society research that Gen Z reports the highest stress and lowest hope of any generation, and links this to rising interest in Bible content on TikTok. Source: https://carmenhansen.substack.com/p/a-digital-revival-the-bibles-unexpected (date not shown)
  - Bustle reported 169M+ engagements on Christian hashtags in 2020, citing Traackr. This figure is dated. Source: https://www.bustle.com/life/christian-tiktok-influencers-gen-z-evangelicalism
- **Synthesis (INFERENCE).** TGB's format fits these patterns:
  - one-line first-person affirmations are easy to identify with and to re-post as a personal statement;
  - they are faceless and cheap to produce at volume (3,000+ IG posts);
  - selling sits in a separate account and the bio link, which avoids the engagement penalty for promotional cues.
